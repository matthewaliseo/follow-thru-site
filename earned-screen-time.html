Earned Screen Time: How It Works and When It Helps

Updated November 21, 2026

Earned screen time flips the usual setup. Instead of a fixed daily allowance, kids start with their apps locked and earn minutes by doing things first: chores, homework, a walk, some push-ups. Here's how it works, where it helps, and where to be careful.

How it works

  1. Pick the apps that wait. Games, video, social apps. Anything you leave off, like Messages, Maps or school apps, stays open.
  2. Set what earns time. A chore might earn 15 minutes. Steps, a workout or a set of push-ups can earn minutes too.
  3. Approve, then they spend. A parent approves the chore. The minutes go onto the child's balance, and they spend them to open their apps.
  4. Time runs out, apps lock. No reminder needed. The apps lock again on their own.

Where it helps

It puts the order of the day into the phone: first the work, then the screen. Kids know exactly what gets them more time, and parents stop being the ones who decide, minute by minute, whether the answer is yes. The answer is already set.

It also counts movement. Children and teens are advised to get an average of 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity a day (World Health Organization guidelines, 2020), and most don't. Earning minutes with steps or push-ups turns some screen time into a reason to move.

Where to be careful

Rewards have a known downside. A large review of the research (Deci, Koestner and Ryan, Psychological Bulletin, 1999) found that expected rewards can lower people's own interest in things they already enjoy. Making screen time the prize can also make it seem more valuable.

So keep it in bounds:

  • Cap it. A daily earning limit keeps a busy Saturday from turning into six hours of games.
  • Don't pay for everything. Keep everyday chores as plain chores if you want. Only some tasks need to earn minutes.
  • Leave some apps open. Messages and school apps shouldn't be a prize.
  • Keep bedtime. Earned minutes shouldn't run past lights out.

Questions

What is earned screen time?

A setup where a child's apps stay locked until they earn minutes by doing things first, like chores, homework or exercise. They spend the minutes to unlock their apps, and the apps lock again when the minutes run out.

Is earned screen time a good idea?

It can be, with limits. Rewards can lower interest in things kids already enjoy (Deci, Koestner and Ryan, 1999), so cap how much can be earned in a day, keep some chores unpaid, and leave school apps and Messages open.

Can adults use earned screen time too?

Yes. CounterWait works for an adult on their own iPhone, or for two adults who check each other, as well as for families.
